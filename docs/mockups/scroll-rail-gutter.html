Sorting by Neighbourhood

Working draft, 2 October 2026

Abstract. Households with similar incomes increasingly live near one another. Using tract-level income distributions for twelve metropolitan areas from 1990 to 2020, I build an index of residential sorting that separates the sorting of households across neighbourhoods from changes in the income distribution itself. Sorting rose in eleven of the twelve cities, by a fifth on average, and most of the rise came between 2000 and 2010.

Introduction

Where a household lives decides more than its commute. It sets the school its children attend, the neighbours it meets, the prices it pays and, through all of these, much of what its children will go on to earn. When households sort by income into separate neighbourhoods, each of these channels carries the advantage or the disadvantage of one generation into the next. Whether that sorting is rising has been argued for as long as it has been measured.

The difficulty is that the usual measures confound two things. An index of segregation computed from tract income shares rises when households of different incomes move apart, but it also rises when the income distribution itself widens and leaves the same tracts further from one another in dollars. Over the last three decades both happened at once, and a rising index tells us little until the two are separated.

This paper separates them. Section 2 describes the tract data for twelve metropolitan areas; Section 3 builds an index that holds the income distribution fixed and estimates how much of each city's change is sorting proper; Section 4 reports the results city by city, and Section 5 asks what they imply for the supply of housing.

1

Three findings stand out. Sorting proper rose in eleven of the twelve cities. The rise was concentrated in a single decade, 2000 to 2010, and was smaller where housing near the centre grew fastest. And the cities in which sorting rose most are not those in which incomes widened most, which is what a story of preferences over neighbours would predict.

Data

The data are tract-level counts of households by income bracket from the decennial censuses of 1990, 2000 and 2010 and the five-year American Community Survey ending in 2020. Tracts are held to their 2010 boundaries throughout, using the published crosswalks.

Sources

Each source reports households in fifteen or sixteen brackets whose limits differ from year to year. I convert every bracket to percentiles of its metropolitan area's distribution in that year, interpolating within brackets under a Pareto assumption for the top bracket and a uniform one elsewhere. The choice matters little: a uniform assumption throughout moves no city's index by more than 0.004.

The sample

The sample is the twelve largest metropolitan areas whose boundaries were stable over the period, taken as their 2013 core-based statistical areas. Tracts with fewer than fifty households in any year, mostly parks, airports and institutions, are dropped; they hold less than half a percent of households. The final sample has 11,416 tracts and about 14.2 million households in 2020.

Table 1, in Section 4, lists the cities with their tract counts.

Method

The approach is to compare each city's observed distribution of households over tracts with the one it would have had if every household kept its rank in its city but incomes were drawn from one fixed distribution.

An index of sorting

Let p index percentiles of income and let sj(p) be the share of tract j's households below percentile p. The sorting index at p is the share of the variance of being below p that lies between tracts,

S(p) = Σj wj (sj(p) − p)² / p(1 − p),(1)

where wj is tract j's share of households. The summary index S averages S(p) over percentiles with weights proportional to p(1 − p), so that neither tail dominates it.

3

Estimation

Because S is computed from percentiles rather than dollars, it does not change under any widening of the income distribution that leaves every household's rank where it was. A rise in S therefore measures households moving apart in rank, not the ranks moving apart in dollars. Standard errors come from a bootstrap over tracts within each city, with 500 replications.

The household-weighted mean of S rose from 0.231 to 0.276, a fifth of its 1990 level. Figure 1 shows that almost half of the rise came in the decade to 2010, and that the cities kept their order throughout.

Where sorting rose least

The three cities with the smallest rise, Portland, Seattle and Minneapolis, share one feature the others lack: each added housing within five miles of its centre faster than at its edge over the period. Across all twelve, the growth of central housing accounts for about 60 percent of the variation in the change in S.

Discussion

If proximity to the centre is scarce and priced, households sort by income because they sort by what they can pay for proximity, whatever they think of their neighbours. The results here fit that account better than one driven by preferences over neighbours: the rise is largest where central housing was scarcest, and it does not follow the widening of incomes. The implication is plain, if not easy. Sorting is lowest where cities let housing grow where people most want to live.
